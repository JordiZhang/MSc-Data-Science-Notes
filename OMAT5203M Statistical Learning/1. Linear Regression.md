We model a linear relationship as 
$$y=\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \epsilon,\qquad \epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$$
With $\beta_0$ being the intercept and $\beta_1$ the gradient. This can of course be extended into multivariate linear regression by having $\beta_0$, $\beta_1$ and $x$ be vectors.

As is usual, we define a loss function. In this case it is natural to use the MSE, which simply measures the deviation of the points from the fitted line. 
$$L(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-(\beta_0 +\beta_1 x_i))^2$$
Minimizing the loss leads us to the following best parameters:
$$\hat{\beta}_0=\bar{y}-\hat{\beta}_1\bar{x},\qquad \hat{\beta}_1=\frac{s_{xy}}{s_x^2}$$
These are in fact equal to the maximum likelihood estimators as we will see in the following section.
# Likelihood
The likelihood is just the probability of generating the observed data using our model. In the linear regression case, each data point is modelled as independent samples from a normal distribution with mean that is a linear function of the input.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}\left(\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma\right) & =P\left(\text { Data } \mid \beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma\right) \\
& =P\left(Y=y \mid X=x, \beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma\right) \\
& =\mathcal{N}\left(y ; \beta_0+\beta_1 x, \sigma^2\right) \\
& =\prod_{i=1}^n \mathcal{N}\left(y_i ; \beta_0+\beta_1 x_i, \sigma^2\right) .
\end{aligned}
$$
It is usually easier and more practical to work with the log likelihood, so:
$$
\log \mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^n \log \mathcal{N}\left(y_i ; \beta_0+\beta_1 x_i, \sigma^2\right) 
$$
Now we can maximize the log-likelihood to find the MLE estimators.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\log \mathcal{L}\left(\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma\right) & =\sum_{i=1}^n \log \mathcal{N}\left(y_i ; \beta_0+\beta_1 x_i, \sigma^2\right) \\
& =\sum_{i=1}^n \log \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma}} \exp \left(\frac{-\left(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1 x_i\right)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\right)\right) \\
& =\sum_{i=1}^n-\frac{\left(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1 x_i\right)^2}{2 \sigma^2}-n \log (\sigma)-\frac{n}{2} \log 2 \pi 
\end{aligned}
$$
Differentiating and setting to 0.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac{\partial \log \mathcal{L}}{\partial \beta_0}\right|_{\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1, \hat{\sigma}} & =0, \\
\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\left(y_i-\hat{\beta}_0-\hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right)}{\hat{\sigma}^2} & =0 \\
\sum_{i=1}^n\left(y_i-\hat{\beta}_0-\hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right) & =0 \\
\Rightarrow \hat{\beta}_0 & =\bar{y}-\hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} 
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac{\partial \log \mathcal{L}}{\partial \beta_1}\right|_{\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1, \hat{\sigma}}=0 . \\
\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\left(y_i-\hat{\beta}_0-\hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right) x_i}{\hat{\sigma}^2}=0 \\
\sum_{i=1}^n\left(y_i-\hat{\beta}_0-\hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right) x_i=0 \\
\overline{y x}-\hat{\beta}_0 \bar{x}-\hat{\beta}_1 \overline{x^2}=0 \\
\overline{y x}-\bar{y} \bar{x}+\hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}^2-\hat{\beta}_1 \overline{x^2}=0, \quad\left[\text { using } \hat{\beta}_0=\bar{y}-\hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\right] \\
\Rightarrow \hat{\beta}_1=\frac{\overline{y x}-\bar{y} \bar{x}}{\overline{x^2}-\bar{x}^2} \\
=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(y, x)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)} 
\end{aligned}
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\left.\frac{\partial \log \mathcal{L}}{\partial \sigma}\right|_{\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1, \hat{\sigma}}=0 .\\

&\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\left(y_i-\hat{\beta}_0-\hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right)^2}{\hat{\sigma}^3} & -\frac{n}{\hat{\sigma}}=0 \\
& \Rightarrow \hat{\sigma}^2=\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n\left(y_i-\hat{\beta}_0-\hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right)^2
\end{aligned}
\end{aligned}
$$
# MLE Estimators
1. $\hat{\beta}_1=\frac{\bar{y} x-\bar{y} \bar{x}}{\bar{x}^2-\bar{x}^2}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(y, x)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}$,
2. $\hat{\beta}_0=\bar{y}-\hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}$,
3. $\hat{\sigma}^2=\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n\left(y_i-\hat{\beta}_0-\hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right)^2$
## Confidence Intervals
When calculating MLE estimates of the parameters, we usually are also interested in the standard error and confidence interval. Recall that the 95% confidence interval for the sample mean of a dataset is:
$$\text{sample mean} \pm \text{standard error} \times t_{2.5\%,n-1}$$
We use the $t$ distribution when sample sizes are small ($n<50$). As the sample size increases, the $t$ distribution tends to the standard one. We use $n-1$ degrees of freedom because when we estimate the sample variance, we construct it from $n-1$ independent data points and the mean. Given the mean and $n-1$ data points, we can always find the $n$-th point. 

In the case of linear regression coefficients, we further reduce the number of independent points to $n-2$. Given $n-2$ data points and the estimates for $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ we can always find the $n-1$ and $n$ points.
# Extending Linear Regression
One way to extend the functionality of a linear regression is to use basis functions. That is to transform the inputs through some non-linear function into new inputs and then perform linear regression as usual. This can also be used in multiple linear regression. A common approach is to model data as a linear sum of polynomial basis terms.
$$
y=\sum_{i=0}^p \beta_i x^i+\epsilon, \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma^2\right) .
$$
