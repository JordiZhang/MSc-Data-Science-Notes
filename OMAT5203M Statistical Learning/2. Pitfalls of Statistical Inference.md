# Multicollinearity
Consider a dataset with multiple inputs, such that two inputs are strongly correlated with each other. In such a case, the outputs can be explained equally well by either input or a suitable combination of them, meaning that you are unable to determine which input explains the outputs. 

Variance inflation factor (VIF) can be used to measure linear dependence or multicollinearity of the inputs. In particular, the VIF measures how much the variance of an estimated regression coefficient increases due to multicollinearity of input variables, thus if multiple inputs are strongly correlated, the resulting regression may have very poor estimates. VIF values are computed for each input $x_i$, for $i=1, \ldots, p$ by fitting a linear regression model separately for each input using the remaining inputs as predictors. The values are defined as:
$$
\mathrm{VIF}_i=\frac{1}{1-R_i^2} .
$$
Where,
$$
R_i^2=1-\frac{\sum_n\left(f_n-y_n\right)^2}{\sum_n\left(y_n-\bar{y}\right)^2},
$$
is the coefficient of determination, the proportion of the variation in the output that is explainable by the input(s), for each model, $i \in\{1, \ldots, p\}$. Here, $f_n$ is the predicted value for $y_n$ and $\bar{y}$ denotes the average of the outputs. For high VIF values (e.g. $>10$ ) the inputs exhibit multicollinearity. The numerator in $R^2$ is the sum of square residuals, while the denominator is the total sum of squares (sum of square deviations from the mean).
# Statistical Inference with Multicollinearity
When inputs are independent, then individual effects of each input can be quantified by computing the expected change in the output when one of the inputs change by one unit and keeping other inputs fixed. Such interpretations are made for coefficients that are statistically significantly different from zero. However, if inputs are correlated, changing an input by one unit would likely lead to other inputs changing by some unknown amount, thus undermining the results. In addition, estimation of the coefficients in the group is not accurate and the fitted coefficients are likely to substantially vary for slightly different datasets from the use of techniques such as cross-validation or bootstrapping. 

Because the fitted coefficients in a group of two or more correlated inputs may not be estimated accurately and interpreting individual coefficients in the group is not appropriate, **group effects** may be computed to carry out statistical inferences. The idea is we consider joint changes in the inputs rather than individual ones. This way, the average group effect is meaningful even when individually none of the coefficients in the group can be accurately estimated.

When computing the (average) group effects, the inputs need to be standardized. The computation of group effects assumes that the inputs are positively correlated because we are assuming each input changes by the same positive amount and investigating the output. Now we can fit the standardized inputs with linear regression. Then the group effects may be computed by averaging the fitted coefficients for each input. Thus depending on the sign and magnitude of it, we can carry out statistical inference. However, we still need to determine whether the group effect is statistically significant from zero. To do so, we can compute an empirical confidence interval using cross-validation or bootstrap. If the interval contains zero, then the group effect is not significantly different from zero, otherwise it is.

Note: The group effect is the overall change in the output when all the inputs are jointly changed after standardizing. In particular, the sum or average of the regression coefficients after standardizing.
